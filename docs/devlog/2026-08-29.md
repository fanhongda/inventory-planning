# 2026-08-29 — master data required, quality gates, S&OP review loop

Four changes, requested together: make the item master mandatory, gate the run on data
quality, produce a forecast a sales organisation can review and take the review back,
and segment the whole thing by business unit / product line / country.

## The item master became required, and why it is two capabilities

`item_dimension` was optional. What a run without it fell back to was not a thinner
number but a different one in the same typeface — MOQ from a config default, obsolete
items replenished against their own history, product family guessed from the SKU
prefix. None of them are visible in the output.

`product_dimension` is deliberately **separate** from `item_dimension`, because only
one of the two fails silently. A master with no MOQ column leaves `min_order_qty` empty
and every consumer can see it. A master with no family column leaves nothing empty:
`assemble._infer_family` fills it from the part number, and a revenue-by-family table
built on a numbering scheme is indistinguishable from one built on master data.

**Rejected: marking `product_family` as `required: true` on the field.** It is the
obvious move and it is wrong. Required fields carry 75% of the routing score in
`registry.py`, so demanding one halves the score of a master that lacks it — the file
then routes to a different contract entirely and the run reports the wrong problem
("your item master was read as a planner worksheet") instead of the real one. The
capability mechanism is decoupled from routing, which is why the gate lives there.

**Cost paid immediately:** 23 tests broke, every one of them a pipeline run whose
fixture had no master. That is the correct blast radius — the fixtures were asserting
the permissive contract — but it is worth knowing that adding a required capability is
never a one-line change.

## Frozen adapters are immune to contract growth

Adding `business_unit` and `product_family` to the master contracts did nothing for a
source with a frozen adapter, because a frozen adapter **is** the column map rather
than a set of overrides on top of the aliases. A field added to the contract later is
never picked up.

This is the cost of the freeze mechanism and it is not documented anywhere else. Every
time the contracts grow, every frozen adapter needs revisiting or it silently keeps
serving the old shape. Worth a check that compares a frozen adapter's map against its
contract's fields and reports what it is not carrying — not built yet.

## Quality gates

New: `inventory_planning/quality/`. Four checkpoints — intake, demand, forecast, plan —
each returning findings with three parts: what is wrong, what it does to the report,
what to do about it. A finding that names a symptom and no action is not worth stopping
a run for.

The hard part was not the blocking, it was the **not** blocking. `_check_quality` had
been advisory since the beginning for a good reason: real exports are dirty, and a gate
that stops on ordinary dirt gets deleted within a week. The distinction the module
settles on is not clean/dirty but *visible/invisible*:

    a field 8% wrong      shows in the report as a finding    → WARN
    a field 100% wrong    is the wrong column, uniformly      → BLOCK
    a join matching 3%    every derived figure reads as zero  → BLOCK

`semantic_failure_ceiling` at 0.50 is what encodes it. Below that, an assertion failure
is data; above it, it is mapping.

**Dimension folding was split into report-and-apply, deliberately.** Folding without
reporting merges two of somebody's business units on the pipeline's own authority.
Reporting without folding leaves every rollup split while the console claims to have
noticed. Both halves, or neither.

## S&OP worksheet and the review loop

Out: history and forecast side by side, **in units and in money**, segmented, as xlsx
with empty adjustment columns. Money is the point — a planner argues about units and a
sales manager argues about revenue, and a forecast in units alone is reviewed by nobody.

Three decisions worth recording:

**The forward amount is computed, not forecast.** `forecast_qty × ASP` rather than a
separately modelled revenue series. Two independent models disagree — the units say 400
and the money says 380 units' worth — and nothing reconciles them.

**The country split is top-down, not a model per cell.** Apportioning the SKU forecast
by each country's share of recent demand cannot see a country diverging from the SKU's
overall trend, which a per-cell model could. What it buys is coherence for free: the
parts sum to the total by construction, so the sales review and the replenishment plan
can never disagree. A bottom-up hierarchy has to be reconciled back, and an
unreconciled one is how a business buys to one number and sells to another.
`split_basis` on every row says which it was, so a cell that earns its own model later
can say so.

**An override replaces the quantity and not the error.** `forecast_rmse` becomes σDL in
the safety stock calculation. A sales adjustment is not evidence that demand became
more predictable — if anything a judgement number is less predictable than a fitted one
— so letting the override reset σ would cut safety stock at exactly the moment the plan
started resting on an opinion. The statistical quantity is kept beside the reviewed one
for the same reason in reverse: a review process that cannot be scored next quarter is
one that never improves.

**The reviewed sheet is loaded by name, not routed by fingerprint.** Every other input
is an extract — a record of something that happened. This one is a decision. It is also
wide with period columns, which is the shape the profiler routes to
`demand_timeseries`. Naming it removes the collision and the risk together: a
spreadsheet that happens to be in an input folder must not be able to overwrite the
demand plan by being profiled.

**A blank cell means no change, not zero.** A reviewer fills two months of six. Reading
the blanks as zeroes would plan four months of no demand for everything on the sheet.

## Three defects only the end-to-end run found

All three passed the unit tests and all three were wrong on a real extract. Worth
remembering that a green suite on synthetic fixtures says nothing about column mapping.

**The money column did not map.** A header of the form `Sales Revenue (USD)` matched no
alias, so every amount on the worksheet was blank and the price summary said "no revenue
column". Cause: single-token aliases are excluded from subset matching by design —
`date` inside `delivery date` is not evidence — so `revenue` alone cannot reach it. The
fix is two-token aliases (`sales revenue`), and the general lesson is that any alias
expected to match a header with a currency or qualifier suffix needs at least two
tokens. The currency suffix is then the unexplained token the penalty exists for, and
the USD column correctly beats the home-currency one because it has fewer of them.

**The business unit never reached the attribute frame.** `assemble.py` propagates a
fixed list of descriptive attributes and `product_family` was on it; the new
`business_unit` was not. So the contract carried it, the adapter mapped it, the gate
checked it — and the rollup was empty. Adding a dimension is a change in at least three
places, and nothing connects them.

**Slow movers lost their geography.** `country_shares` fell back to the whole history
only when the *entire* trailing window was empty, rather than per SKU. An item whose
last sale predates the window got no share and landed on the sheet as "(not stated)" —
a fifth of the rows on the real extract, and exactly the items a reviewer is least sure
about. Fixed per-SKU.

## The family gate came back down, and found a fourth defect

Reversed on instruction: a SKU that sells but has no master row, or no product family,
must **not** stop the run. The report comes out and the missing materials are counted
in the summary.

The reasoning is better than the original. An item selling without a classified master
row is the ordinary state of anything new or transferred in — a planner who cannot get
a report until master data is perfect gets no reports, and a gate that blocks on the
normal state of a live catalogue is a gate that will be switched off. What the original
argument actually demanded was not a stop but a **count**, plus an output a reader can
reconcile that count against. So: two warnings instead of one block, split because the
gaps have different owners — `sku_missing_from_master` needs the item *created*,
`product_family_missing` needs it *classified* — and a new `product_family_source`
column on every row reading `master` or `inferred`.

A wholesale-absent family column still fails the `product_dimension` capability. Some
items unclassified is a warning; no classification at all is not a gap in the data, it
is the absence of the thing being reported on.

**And the tag immediately exposed a silent pre-existing bug.** Every row on a real
extract came out `inferred`, despite a master with a fully populated family column, for
two compounding reasons:

1. `product_family` was read from `item_master` only, never from `planning_master` — and
   a hand-maintained safety stock column is exactly what the discriminator routes on, so
   the common case for a real planner's master is the one that was excluded. The field
   was mapped, contract-tested and gated, then never read.
2. `_infer_family` ran *before* `_merge_masters`' descriptive merge. The guess left no
   nulls, and that merge is a `.where(notna)` which therefore never fired. A master
   value arriving late lost to a guess arriving early, silently, for all 1,246 SKUs.

Either alone would have been survivable; together they discarded the master's
classification entirely while the output looked complete. This is the fourth defect
today that unit tests passed and real data caught, and the pattern in all four is the
same: **a fallback that fires before the real value arrives is indistinguishable from
the real value.** Order of resolution is not a detail.

## Three severities, because two collapsed the case that mattered

Reversed again, and the second reversal is the more interesting one: a master with **no
product family column at all** should not block either.

The argument that settled it is that replenishment does not read the family. Safety
stock, reorder points, order quantities and every per-SKU recommendation come out
byte-identical with the family and without it. What the family decides is how results
are *grouped*. Withholding a plan a buyer can act on because the rollup above it would
be unreliable helps nobody — and the buyer is the person the run exists for.

But an ordinary warning was equally wrong. Sitting in a list of eleven notes, it gets
skimmed, and the revenue-by-product-line table gets presented at a meeting as if it
meant something. So a third severity, and the axis it splits on is **what a reader has
to do**, not how bad the data is:

    BLOCK    stops the run          damage a reader cannot see from the output
    SEVERE   leads every output     specific figures are not what they appear
    WARN     a count or a repair    context to note and move past

`no_product_dimension` is SEVERE. It carries an `impacts` list naming the tables that
are now grouping on the part-number prefix — and, deliberately, a **"Not affected"**
line: safety stock, reorder points, purchase quantities. Without that line the warning
is read as "the whole report is suspect", the recommendations get discarded with the
rollup, and the run has cost the planner something instead of telling them something.
A warning that does not bound itself is worse than a quieter one.

## Findings had to be restated where the outputs are

Every finding was printed at the moment it was raised, which is the wrong moment for
all but the first. By the time a planner reaches the purchase recommendations, sixty
lines of intake output have scrolled past, and the sentence that would have changed how
they read the revenue table went by before there was a revenue table to read.

`quality/health.py` collects the gate findings and the capability degradations — two
different kinds of statement, *something is wrong* and *something is absent*, but a
reader needs both for the same purpose and two incomplete places is how one gets missed
— and restates them ranked at the end of the run, as `run_health_<run>.md`, and as
`quality_gates_<run>.json` for the report builder.

One formatting lesson worth keeping: the impacts were first rendered as a two-column
table by splitting each on its first colon. It worked for the impacts written as
"output: effect" and mangled the ones that were not — a formatter deciding the shape of
a sentence. They are a bulleted list now, and the structured JSON is what any visual
layout should read.

## Report: health first, then three aggregate views

Four additions, and three of them turn on the same question — what is the right way to
aggregate a per-SKU number to a line or a period.

**Health banner, above the KPI tiles.** Severe findings expanded with their impacts;
warnings and missing inputs counted on one line. A reader who meets the numbers first
has formed a view by the time the caveat arrives. The `Not affected` line is styled
apart from the damage, for the same reason it exists at all.

**S&IOP — demand COGS, supply and gap by month.** The first aggregate view in the
pipeline; everything else is per SKU and point-in-time. Two decisions worth keeping:

- Demand is valued at **cost** here and at **selling price** on the S&OP worksheet. The
  first asks what the business must buy and hold, the second is the number sales
  review. Both correct, not comparable, and each says which it is on its face.
- The projection runs per SKU and is **summed**, never computed on the aggregate.
  Netting first lets one item's surplus cover another's shortage and reports a business
  in balance that cannot ship an order. And a period is short where the projected close
  falls below **safety stock**, not below zero — service is at risk before the shelf is
  bare.

**Forecast accuracy — rebuilt, twice.** First on backtest MAPE, value-weighted. Both
halves of that were wrong, and the correction is the sharpest point of the day.

*Wrong source.* The backtest asks whether the model was the best available for a
series — the modeller's question, and answerable on the first run because it never
leaves the history. The S&IOP meeting asks something else entirely: it published a
number, often after sales changed it, and the business bought stock and set targets
against it. A model can win its backtest and still be wrong about next March, and a
reviewed forecast that overrode the model **is not in the backtest at all**. So
accuracy is now the published plan against what then sold. It needs history: each run
stores its plan in the snapshot, a later run scores the periods that have closed, and
on a first run the section says it cannot measure yet rather than substituting a
statistic that would look like an answer.

Where two runs planned the same period, the **earliest** is scored. That is the number
the business acted on; measuring the revision published a fortnight later with more
information would flatter every forecast.

*Wrong shape.* Even value-weighted, a single figure is a scoreboard, and a scoreboard
closes a discussion — "MAPE was 34%" tells nobody what to do differently, everyone nods,
the meeting moves on. The same data ranked by **where the plan missed by the most
money** opens one: this line ran forty per cent under all half-year, whose assumption
was that, what changes. So the output is a ranking of lines and items, not a mean.

Ranking by value rather than percentage survives from the first version and is the one
thing that was right: on the fixture, a part missed by 300% ranks below a line missed by
43%, because the money is five hundred times larger.

This also required a fix underneath: the snapshot stored only `forecast_next_period`,
which after an override *is* the reviewed figure, so the statistical one was lost and
"did the adjustment help?" was permanently unanswerable — and it stored t+1 only, so
nothing beyond next month could ever be scored. It now stores the whole published
horizon with both numbers. A review process that cannot be scored never improves.

**DIOH by product line, value-weighted for the same reason.** One dead part holding
forty dollars scores 12,000 days; averaging per-SKU DIOH lets it speak as loudly as a
live item holding forty thousand, and on any real catalogue the dead ones are the
majority. Total value over total daily COGS is what the money actually does.

Ageing is the honest half. Most stock extracts carry no age — the real one gives
opening, receipts, issues and closing, and nothing else — so `inventory_age_days` and
`last_movement_date` were added to the contract as optional, and where neither arrives
the analysis falls back to **days of cover under its own name**. The two are wrong
about each other in both directions: a fast item restocked yesterday shows a year of
cover and no age, an ancient part with one recent order shows almost no cover. Labelling
cover as age would put the wrong items on a write-off list.

Two bugs found by looking at the rendered page rather than the tests: `_pct` formats
with `:%` and so takes a *fraction*, and every new call site was passing it a
percentage — "7% of stock" rendered as 673%. And the YAML for the ageing fields went in
before the first `assertions:` in the file, which was one nested inside a field rather
than the top-level block; the contract stopped parsing, and the failure surfaced as
"missing required capabilities" rather than as a broken contract. Worth fixing that
error path separately.

## Still open

- Which price basis the forward amount should use was never settled — trailing 12-month
  realised ASP is the default in place, with the basis recorded per row.
- Per-cell country forecasting, where a cell has enough history to earn one.
- A check that a frozen adapter still covers its contract's fields.
- `_check_quality`'s interactive prompt is now redundant with the gates and should
  probably go.
